5  Signal Typing

Note

Living draft. This chapter presents the classification that gates every subsequent measure. Later chapters cross-reference the three types by number throughout their Caveats sections. Found an error? Use the Report an issue link in the sidebar.

5.1 Why signal typing comes first

Fundamental frequency, jitter, shimmer, and harmonics-to-noise ratio all presuppose an identifiable repeating waveform. Disordered voices routinely violate that assumption. Applying a perturbation formula to a signal that has no periodic structure produces a number, but not a meaningful one (Baken and Orlikoff 2000). A three-category classification of voiced signals, originally proposed by Titze (1995) at the NCVS Workshop on Acoustic Voice Analysis and formalised for the clinic by Baken and Orlikoff (2000), addresses this by naming which kinds of vocal signal support which kinds of measurement.

Every measure chapter in this reference relies on the classification. This chapter is the single authority those cross-references resolve to.

5.2 The three types

The classification, with clinical subtypes added by Baken and Orlikoff (2000) shown in italics:

5.2.1 Type 1 — nearly periodic (or, in the ideal case, periodic)

Waveforms that do not undergo qualitative changes during the analysis interval. Any modulating or subharmonic frequencies present carry energy at least one order of magnitude below the fundamental (Baken and Orlikoff 2000).

  • Subtype A: minor random variation of F0 or waveshape.
  • Subtype B: minor monotonic modulation — F0 or amplitude steadily increasing or decreasing.
  • Subtype C: minor cyclic variation (modulation) of F0 or amplitude.

5.2.2 Type 2 — organised but non-stationary

Signals with sudden qualitative changes (bifurcations), or with modulating or subharmonic frequencies whose energy is comparable to that of the fundamental. The signal is clearly ordered and organised, but no single F0 value validly characterises the entire segment (Baken and Orlikoff 2000).

  • Subtype A: signal bifurcation(s) present.
  • Subtype B: F0 or amplitude discontinuity.
  • Subtype C: large-scale modulation of F0.

5.2.3 Type 3 — without apparent periodicity

Signals without apparent periodicity, and hence not validly susceptible to F0 measurement. Type 3 signals may nonetheless be chaotic in the technical sense — carrying a covert organising principle discoverable through dynamical-systems techniques (Baken and Orlikoff 2000).

  • Subtype A: no observable structure.
  • Subtype B: deep-level (chaotic) organisation.

5.3 Applying the classification

Signal typing is a visual-inspection step performed before perturbation measurement, not a numeric procedure. The vocal waveform (or a close correlate, such as an electroglottographic trace) is displayed and examined for the presence of bifurcations, modulations, discontinuities, and periodicity. The analysis window is chosen so that a single type applies across it (Baken and Orlikoff 2000). A computer-based analysis system will happily calculate a putative F0 for any signal, including aperiodic ones, but the number produced has no meaning if the signal is Type 3 (Baken and Orlikoff 2000).

The classification is signal-based, not disorder-based. A single speaker may produce all three types across a session, and the same disorder can present differently at different F0 or intensity levels. The underlying mathematical distinction is periodicity itself: a waveform is periodic when f(t + T) = f(t), so the waveform is an exact copy of itself after some period T has elapsed (Titze 2000). Any departure from this exact-repetition condition is aperiodicity of some kind.

5.4 Which measures apply to which types

Signal type gates the measures that follow in Parts II–IV.

Type Reliable Marginal Unreliable / undefined
Type 1 Jitter, shimmer, HNR, CPPS, spectral tilt, F0, all composites — —
Type 2 CPPS, some spectral measures HNR, spectral tilt Jitter, shimmer, composite indices
Type 3 Cepstral-domain measures (CPPS); dynamical-systems methods — Any measure that requires F0 tracking
  • Perturbation measures require Type 1 signals. For F0 evaluation to be meaningful, the signal should within reasonable limits be classifiable as Type 1, subtype A (Baken and Orlikoff 2000). Occasional discontinuities may be less damaging than once believed for some software (Baken and Orlikoff 2000), but the general rule stands.
  • Noise measures (HNR, NHR) presuppose a discernible periodic component to separate from the noise; they are unreliable on Type 2 and undefined on Type 3.
  • Cepstral peak prominence is robust across signal types. CPP and CPPS do not require pitch detection or tracking and can be extracted from connected speech as well as sustained vowels. This is one of the practical reasons ASHA recommended CPP as a general dysphonia measure in preference to jitter, shimmer, and HNR (see the CPPS chapter).
  • Composite indices inherit their components’ signal-type constraints. Because AVQI and ABI include perturbation and HNR-like terms, they are sensitive to non-Type-1 material; see the AVQI and ABI chapters.
  • Type 3 signals need dynamical-systems methods, not periodicity-based measures, and that direction of investigation is one of the frontier areas in acoustic voice analysis (Baken and Orlikoff 2000).

The practical workflow: display the signal, classify it, and if it is not Type 1 either shorten the analysis window until a Type 1 segment is isolated, switch to a signal-type-robust measure such as CPPS, or report the signal as unanalyzable by perturbation methods — rather than reporting a spurious number.

5.5 Caveats

  • The classification is human-judgment-based. Two clinicians may disagree at the boundaries, particularly between Type 1 subtype C and Type 2 subtype C (both involve F0 modulation; they differ in the magnitude relative to the fundamental) (Baken and Orlikoff 2000).
  • Software rarely refuses to compute perturbation values on non-Type-1 signals — the user must apply the type check themselves.
  • The analysis window matters. A speaker who produces Type 2 material during a phrase transition and Type 1 material during a sustained vowel is both, depending on which window is analysed.
  • The Baken and Orlikoff subtypes (A, B, C) are their own extension of Titze (1995)’s three-type framework; other authors have used other subdivisions or none at all. Cite the subtype only where the sensitivity of the analysis warrants it.
